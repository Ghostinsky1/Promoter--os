Live Nation's concert business runs on 2.8%. One promoter made 39% on a $641 headliner.

Artist fees are the top squeeze on independent promoters in 2026. One settlement sheet shows where the money actually goes.

By Jose Huaroco, Founder, Promoter OS · September 21, 2026 · 6 min read

Published by Promoter OS. The author founded the company, and the show data below is his own.

The biggest concert promoter on earth had a record year in 2024 — and kept less than three cents of every dollar.

Live Nation's concerts division brought in $19.02 billion in revenue, and its adjusted operating margin reached a record 2.8%, according to Billboard.

Independent promoters are doing worse. A study by the National Independent Venue Association found 64% of independent venues surveyed were unprofitable in 2024, and just under a third of their expenses went directly to artist and booking fees.

It isn't easing. In NIVA's first Live Pulse survey this June, more than 212 independent venues, promoters and festivals reported softening advance ticket demand on one side and climbing costs on the other. Ticket sales and artist fees ranked as the top two pressures — a combination the survey called “a structural trap.”

All agents look at their artist as a product. And as a product, the market decides your worth. Jose Huaroco

The settlement

Huaroco opened the books on one night this summer: a 1,000-capacity underground room in the Midwest, Latin rave format.

Venue−$3,057
Headliner−$641
Two support DJs−$500

Profit$3,319

The headliner was 7.5% of revenue, and a 39% margin on the night. All talent combined was 22% of costs — well under the national average for independents.

Ad-spend note: $413 is the spend tied to this show on receipts. At Huaroco's full $1,500 limit, profit falls to $2,232 — still 26%.

Did you just fund a vacation?

Huaroco's test takes ten seconds. Divide the artist's fee by what each attendee is worth after costs — about $20 a head on this night. The answer is how many people the artist has to bring who wouldn't have come anyway.

250 people he has to bring who weren't coming anyway — just to cover himself
Are they going to sell at least their cost in tickets? Or did you just fund a vacation? Jose Huaroco

His checks before signing: pull the artist's last ten dates. If they were the support act on most of them, they are not a headliner yet. Then estimate their pay — ticket price, times roughly how many came, times 40%. He treats that as a floor, since it runs low on soft nights.

Why fees keep climbing

Veteran venue managers say headline talent fees have jumped 30–40% since 2020, and a single bad deal can wipe out profits from several good nights. Major promoters are bidding aggressively for artists' limited touring dates, sometimes pricing independents out entirely. And 60% of venues surveyed by NIVA expected artist fees to rise again in 2025.

Huaroco argues the price an agent quotes is a national number, while promoters sell tickets in one city.

LA can't get enough. But no one in KC cares. Jose Huaroco

The cost nobody negotiates

The largest expense on Huaroco's night wasn't the artist. The venue cost $3,057 — 4.8 times the headliner.

At half a house — roughly 213 people and $4,250 in revenue — that room loses money even with a free headliner.

I went back and forth for weeks over $641. I signed the room in an afternoon. Jose Huaroco

What experienced promoters do differently

A common rule among promoters: flat guarantees are for when you're 90%+ confident in turnout, and splits or versus deals are for the uncertain cases.

Huaroco's own record doesn't follow it. All seven of his settled shows were flat guarantees. He says that's the first thing he's changing, along with:

  • Judging every deal at half a house instead of a sellout.
  • Settling the venue and the ad budget before the artist fee.
  • Negotiating the merch table. At the 200 to 1,500 capacity level, merch routinely outearns ticket revenue for the artist; on the night above, 100% of it went to the performer.

He isn't against paying artists. He's against paying them for a crowd they didn't bring.

If you want to be flown out, maybe your name should sell tickets. But we need artists who grow with us, too. In this industry we need both. Jose Huaroco

How these numbers were built

Revenue from ticketing platform payouts, door cash and direct checkout deposits. Costs from the venue's closeout sheet, payment records and ad receipts. The $5,000 scenario was run in Promoter OS with every other cost left unchanged. Artist, venue and exact date withheld to protect the parties involved.
